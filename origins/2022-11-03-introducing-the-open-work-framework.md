Open Work Framework (OWF) is a concept designed to offer the working class the profits of their work.

For a background on why this framework is important, read this story first: [Why jobs don’t make sense anymore for the working class.](2022-10-25-why-jobs-dont-make-sense.md)

There are many things to be taken into consideration while designing the framework. For entrepreneurs, what starts off as an idea is converted to a vision, and brought to fruition. But the basic premise is that in the world of business, ideas mean nothing until they are implemented and executed.

*[Image: Screenshot of a working proof of concept for open work framework.]*

So a basic idea, when implemented, first needs to break down into tasks and sub-tasks and then some more ideas, and assigned to people to work on. Each idea has some value, but the tasks done on them have more value as compared to the concept of the idea.

OWF attributes ideas to 1% of the work done, and rest everything to 99% of the work done.

You could simply think of it as inspired from the quote “Genius is 1% inspiration and 99% perspiration”.

It works like this:

Whoever comes up with an idea, or adds value to it by creating tasks will be directly attributed 1% of the profits, without a doubt.

And the rest 99% is attributed to people who start working on the idea. Work includes anything and everything being done to bring the idea to fruition. From operations to sales to accounting to designing, investment, networking, and lots more, everything is work.

How to distribute this 99% to different types of tasks is the real question.

The simple answer is by finding and calculating the contribution of each task performed by the team or an individual, and linking it to profits of the business.

But it’s not easy to link the profits directly to tasks. One way is to set up several metrics and KPIs (Key Performance Indicators) together to find the link, but even then it’ll be very difficult. Because then, metrics are needed for every single task out of thousands of tasks being performed. And it’s not plain easy to set up metrics for everything.

For example, a team picked up the task of marketing. They came up with some cool designs, and ran campaigns on different platform. All such platforms are then equipped with conversion metrics, and the conversion funnel is linked to direct & indirect purchases that the targeted customers make. But the product/service that the customer purchases is built by a different team. And then the support is provided by a different team altogether. Even if you combine the metrics together from different departments of work, even then it is cumbersome to correctly evaluate their contribution towards the profits. Talk about a small business trying to setup all these metric funnels in the first month of their business. Hence, KPIs are not enough.

In short, several factors combined together contribute to the profits of a business.

Let’s try putting in the current way of defining salaries for employees of different departments. Why are software developers paid a certain amount and graphic designers a different amount? It is all based on the experience and competencies of the individuals. Yes?

No. It doesn’t work like that.

Whatever an individual earns is based on the negotiations they do before commencing the job. A good negotiator earns way more than a bad negotiator, regardless of the quality and quantity of work they do. Frankly, the whole world works on negotiations and influence. If you’re neither of both, you will have a hard time earning the big bucks.

Even if it is based on experience and competencies, it is not calculated. The job of deciding salaries is assigned to a person sitting above in the hierarchical order.

## So how does OWF work?

The person or the team coming up with tasks to be performed on the ideas will be the ones assigned to pre-define the complexity of those tasks. The complexity could be on a scale of 1 to 10, based on some calculations that depend on a number of circumstances.

For simplicity, let’s have the task originators assign the complexity based on their opinions. When each task has been agreed upon a certain complexity level, people pick up the tasks and start working on them. One task could be picked up by one person or more than one person, given the information of who is working on what is transparent. They could agree to work with each other or separately, and then submit the work.

Once submitted, the work is evaluated and approved, or rejected until it meets the standards required. The approval again has to be done by the originators of the tasks. If approved, the total work done based on the complexity is added to a weighted distribution system of several tasks.

Based on the weighted distribution system then, the profits of each month of the complete product/service is calculated and distributed to everyone who worked on the tasks.

## Capital

Whoever starts a project in this framework could come up with preset pool of employee profits distribution and a corpus capital required for the project. A 70–30 or 60–40 distribution should be recommended to sustain projects in the long-term.

*[Image: profit distribution pool]*

Company corpus capital could be used for other expenses, and need not be necessarily at 30%. It could be at any percentage.

## Profits

Profits, when calculated, are assigned to each tasks even before they are taken up and worked on. Of course they change over time as more and more tasks take place in the project. After all, all the tasks combined together make up the complete project.

Every task has a profit percentage assigned to it. So the people assigned to those tasks get the profits till the project is earning profits. A person could have worked on one or more tasks, summing up to their monthly earnings from the project. Just like a company would pay dividends to its shareholders based on the amount of capital they put in.

## Calculation

Let c = complexity of a task,
P = Total project profits for the month
S = Sum of all task complexities

```
Profits of a task = (c * P) / S
```
